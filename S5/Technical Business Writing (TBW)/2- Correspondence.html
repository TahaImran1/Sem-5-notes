Technical & Business Writing (TBW) — Module 2

Correspondence: Letters, Memos & Email

Primary Textbook: William S. Pfeiffer, Technical Communication: A Practical Approach (8th Ed.), Chapter 6

1General Principles & ABC Message Patterns

Planning correspondence, the ABC Diamond structure, and strategic message patterns

Workplace correspondence consists of written messages sent to individuals or small groups. It comprises three primary vehicles: Letters (external formal), Memos (internal formal/semi-formal), and E-mail (electronic internal or external).

1. Planning Your Correspondence (Pfeiffer Guidelines)

  • Determine Your Reader: Who is the primary decision-maker? Who might receive courtesy copies (CC)? What is their technical level and existing knowledge?
  • Determine Your Purpose: Are you delivering good news, bad news, routine data, or requesting action?
  • Determine Your Scope: Include only what the reader needs to act. Avoid cluttering the page with irrelevant background.

2. The Universal ABC Format

Pfeiffer organizes all technical correspondence using the ABC Format (Abstract, Body, Conclusion).

The ABC Diamond Structure
  • Abstract (A): The lead-in / orientation. States clearly why you are writing, provides immediate context, and reveals the "bottom line" (what the document is about).
  • Body (B): The detailed discussion. Contains the evidence, technical specifications, background facts, and figures. Broken up visually with bullet points, numbered lists, or bold subheadings.
  • Conclusion (C): The forward-looking action step. Tells the reader what happens next, states deadlines or specific requested actions, and ends with professional goodwill.

3. Strategic Message Patterns (Positive, Negative, Neutral, Persuasive)

How you sequence the ABC format depends entirely on how the reader is expected to react to your news:

Positive Correspondence (Direct Plan)

Used when the reader will be pleased or eager to receive your message (e.g., job offer, refund granted, promotion).

  • Abstract: State the good news or approval immediately.
  • Body: Give necessary details, terms, explanations, or instructions.
  • Conclusion: Express warm, friendly goodwill and look forward to future collaboration.

Negative Correspondence (Indirect Plan)

Used when delivering unwelcome news (e.g., claim refusal, credit denial, contract rejection).

  • Abstract (Buffer): Open with a neutral, pleasant statement that sets common ground without falsely promising approval.
  • Body (Reasons First): Explain the objective facts, company policies, or constraints before stating the refusal.
  • De-emphasized Refusal: State the bad news gently and diplomatically (often combined with a constructive alternative).
  • Conclusion: Offer a counterproposal or helpful alternative, closing on genuine goodwill without reopening the negative issue.

Neutral Correspondence (Direct Plan)

Used for routine workplace transactions (e.g., inquiries, acknowledgements, transmittals).

  • Abstract: State the request, questions, or transmittal purpose directly.
  • Body: Present questions or details in an itemized, numbered list for easy reading.
  • Conclusion: Specify the desired response date and explain why that date matters.

Persuasive Correspondence (AIDA Plan)

Used when the reader is indifferent or hesitant (e.g., sales letters, project budget proposals).

  • A — Attention: Open with an engaging hook, compelling statistic, or shared problem.
  • I — Interest: Explain the solution and highlight direct benefits to the reader.
  • D — Desire: Provide credible evidence, testimonials, or data to remove objections.
  • A — Action: Make it effortless for the reader to take the specific next step.
Exam Tip: The Indirect Bad News Formula
FAST and Pfeiffer exams frequently test negative letters! Remember the exact 5-step sequence:
1. Neutral Buffer → 2. Thorough Explanation → 3. Diplomatic Refusal → 4. Constructive Alternative → 5. Forward-Looking Goodwill Close.
Never start a negative letter with "I am writing to inform you that we have rejected your request." That alienates the customer instantly!

Key Takeaways

  • The ABC format adapts to four emotional contexts: Positive, Negative, Neutral, and Persuasive.
  • Good news uses the direct pattern (news first); bad news uses the indirect pattern (buffer and reasons first).
  • Always maintain the "You" attitude and end with a forward-looking action step.

2Business Letters & The 3 Layout Formats

External formal correspondence, the 10 standard parts, and formatting rules

A Business Letter is a formal document sent to individuals outside your organization (clients, suppliers, government agencies, shareholders). It serves as a permanent, legally recognized record of a business transaction.

1. The 10 Standard Components of a Business Letter

According to Pfeiffer (Chapter 6) and university course rubrics, an official business letter consists of the following 10 parts:

Element Description & Placement Exact Example
1. Letterhead / Heading Company logo, company name, address, phone, email, and web address. If using plain paper, type your full return address without your personal name. Avanceon Private Limited
12-B, Main Boulevard, Gulberg-II
Lahore, Pakistan
2. Date Line Placed 2 lines below the letterhead. Never abbreviate the month (avoid 10/24/26 to eliminate global date format confusion). October 24, 2026 (or 24 October 2026)
3. Inside Address Placed 2 to 4 lines below the date. Recipient's courtesy title, full name, professional title, company name, and full postal address. Mr. Adil Rizwan
Manager Customer Support
Social Square
Phase III, DHA, Lahore
4. Subject Line Optional but highly recommended in technical writing. Placed either immediately above or below the salutation. Clearly states the document focus. SUBJECT: Defect in Salary Management Software (Contract #440)
5. Salutation Formal greeting. Always use a colon (:) in formal American business letters (commas are only for informal personal letters). Dear Mr. Rizwan: (Avoid: "To Whom It May Concern")
6. Body Paragraphs Single-spaced within paragraphs, with 1 blank line between paragraphs. Organized using the ABC Format. Abstract (Purpose) → Body (Facts/Impact) → Conclusion (Action/Deadline).
7. Complimentary Close Placed 2 lines below the body. Capitalize only the first word, followed by a comma (in mixed punctuation). Sincerely, or Best regards,
8. Signature Block Leave 3 to 4 blank lines for the physical, handwritten signature in blue or black ink. [Handwritten Signature]
9. Typed Name & Title The writer's full typed name and organizational job designation. Kaleem Ullah
Executive Assistant to CEO
10. End Notations Placed 1 to 2 lines below the typed title. Indicates enclosed documents, typist initials, or courtesy copies. Enclosure: Error Screenshot
cc: Mr. Avais, CEO, Social Square

2. The 3 Standard Letter Layout Formats

In modern business communication, three formats dominate. In all three, text within paragraphs is single-spaced, with double-spacing between paragraphs.

1. Full Block Format

The Most Popular & Contemporary Format.

  • Alignment: Every single line begins flush against the left margin.
  • Indentation: Zero indentation for paragraph beginnings.
  • Punctuation: Usually pairs with Open Punctuation (no punctuation after salutation or complimentary close) or Mixed Punctuation (colon after salutation, comma after close).
  • Advantage: Fastest to type; modern, sleek aesthetic.
[Letterhead]
[Date]
[Inside Address]
[Salutation:]
[Body - flush left]
[Body - flush left]
[Complimentary Close,]
[Signature]
[Typed Name]

2. Modified Block Format

The Traditional Balanced Format.

  • Alignment: The Date, Complimentary Close, and Signature Block start at the horizontal center of the page (tabbed to center).
  • Inside Address & Salutation: Flush left.
  • Paragraphs: Block style (no paragraph indentation).
  • Punctuation: Typically uses Mixed Punctuation (colon after salutation, comma after close).
                  [Date]
[Inside Address]
[Salutation:]
[Body - flush left]
[Body - flush left]
                  [Close,]
                  [Signature]
                  [Typed Name]

3. Semi-Block (Indented) Format

The Classic Formal Style.

  • Alignment: Date, Complimentary Close, and Signature Block start at the horizontal center (like Modified Block).
  • Paragraphs: The first line of each paragraph is indented 5 spaces (0.5 inch).
  • Inside Address & Salutation: Flush left.
  • Use Case: Conservative industries, formal academic or diplomatic correspondence.
                  [Date]
[Inside Address]
[Salutation:]
     [Body - indented]
     [Body - indented]
                  [Close,]
                  [Signature]
                  [Typed Name]

Key Takeaways

  • Always identify all 10 standard parts in business letter questions.
  • In Full Block, everything is flush left. In Modified Block, date and close start at horizontal midpoint. In Semi-Block, paragraph first lines are indented.
  • A business letter salutation uses a colon (:), never a comma.
  • Complaint letters must remain objective, factual, and polite while specifying the exact requested remedy and deadline.

3Memorandums (Memos - Internal Correspondence)

In-house correspondence, standard header formatting, and memo types

A Memorandum (Memo) is a written document used exclusively for communication within an organization. It travels upward to management, downward to subordinates, or horizontally to colleagues and peers across departments.

Letter vs. Memo: Key Structural Differences
Feature Business Letter Memorandum (Memo)
Audience External stakeholders (clients, vendors, public). Internal employees, managers, coworkers.
Header Block Letterhead, Date, Inside Address. Standard 4-line block: DATE:, TO:, FROM:, SUBJECT:
Salutation & Close Mandatory (Dear Mr. X: and Sincerely,). Omitted completely! (No "Dear...", no "Sincerely").
Signature Handwritten signature above typed name at bottom. Writer initials their name in the FROM: line at top.
Technical Jargon Minimized or explained for outside readers. Company acronyms and technical terms used freely.

1. The Standard Memo Heading Block

Memos begin with a standardized 4-part heading. The order of these lines may vary slightly by company template, but all four must be present: 

DATE:    October 25, 2026
TO:      Naveed Ahmed, Manager Product Development
FROM:    Adil Rizwan, Manager Customer Support [AR]
SUBJECT: Urgent Complaint Regarding Avanceon Payroll Software Error
Writing Effective Subject Lines
A vague subject line like SUBJECT: Payroll or SUBJECT: Error is unacceptable. A strong subject line combines Topic + Specific Focus: SUBJECT: Payroll Software Error - Investigation Request for Avanceon Account.

2. The 6 Core Purposes of Memos (Pfeiffer)

  • 1. Documenting: Creating an official paper trail of expenses, decisions, software incidents, or personnel actions.
  • 2. Explaining Procedures: Instructing staff on how to use new tools, observe safety standards, or configure servers.
  • 3. Transmittal / Cover: Accompanying attached documents (e.g., formal audit reports or blueprints) and summarizing key findings.
  • 4. Confirming Decisions: Documenting verbal agreements made during meetings or phone calls to prevent future disputes.
  • 5. Feasibility Analysis: Evaluating whether a proposed change (e.g., migrating to cloud servers) is practical and cost-effective.
  • 6. Status & Progress Reporting: Providing weekly, monthly, or quarterly updates on milestone completion and budget burn.

3. Complete Model: Internal Investigation Memo

Model Internal Memo (Social Square Field Visit)
MEMORANDUM

DATE:    October 26, 2026
TO:      Adil Rizwan, Manager Customer Support
FROM:    Amna Ilyas, Secretary to Product Development Manager [AI]
SUBJECT: Findings of On-Site Technical Review at Avanceon Private Limited

[ABSTRACT]
On October 25, 2026, Mrs. Naveed (Product Development Manager) and our technical lead visited the Gulberg offices of Avanceon Private Limited to investigate the reported failure in their Salary Management Program. This memo summarizes our diagnostic findings and encloses the official field audit report for policy action.

[BODY]
During our review of the server event logs, our diagnostic team confirmed that the Salary Management Program database did not suffer from an internal source code bug. Instead, the crash was triggered by an unauthorized external penetration attempt:
• IP Address Tracing: Multiple brute-force login attempts originated from an internal workstation within Avanceon's local subnet (IP 192.168.10.45).
• Security Lockout: Our software's automated security daemon correctly initiated a database lockdown to prevent payroll ledger manipulation, which displayed as a connectivity failure.
• System Integrity: The core payroll data remains uncorrupted and 100% intact.

[CONCLUSION]
Because the shutdown was caused by an internal security breach at the client's premises rather than vendor code error, we recommend the following next steps:
1. Provide Avanceon's IT Director with the enclosed IP log audit so they can isolate the compromised terminal.
2. Issue an Adjustment Letter along with a reset security key to restore full payroll operations immediately.

Enclosure: Field Visit Diagnostic & Security Audit Report (Ref #SR-2026-99)
cc: Mrs. Naveed, Manager Product Development

Key Takeaways

  • Memos are strictly internal; never include salutations (Dear...) or complimentary closings (Sincerely...).
  • The header must always include DATE, TO, FROM, SUBJECT.
  • The writer signifies approval by initialing next to their typed name on the FROM: line.
  • Organize the memo body using ABC format with clear subheadings or bullet points.

4Email Hygiene, Group Rules & The 7 Cs

Speed vs. quality trade-off, Netiquette, group distribution etiquette, and the 7 Cs

Email is the lifeblood of contemporary business communication. However, its greatest strength—instant speed—is also its greatest liability. Careless emails can permanently damage reputations, create legal liabilities, and ruin client relationships.

1. The Speed vs. Quality Dilemma (Pfeiffer)

Core Principle
Balance Speed and Craft: Never hit "Send" in haste or anger. An email that takes 2 minutes to dash off can take months to clean up legally or professionally.

2. Professional Email Hygiene & Netiquette

  • The "Billboard Test": Never put anything in an email that you would not want posted on a public billboard or read aloud in a courtroom. Emails are legally discoverable records.
  • No Flames: A "flame" is an aggressive, sarcastic, or insulting email sent in emotional anger. If you receive a flame, wait at least 24 hours before responding, and always respond with objective facts.
  • No ALL CAPS (Shouting): Writing in all uppercase letters is perceived as shouting and aggressive. Writing in all lowercase looks lazy and illiterate.
  • Professional Usernames: Use official name-based accounts (kullah@avanceon.com), never personal nicknames or hobby aliases (gamer99@domain.com).
  • Eliminate Emoticons & Chat Slang: Keep smileys, emojis, and texting acronyms (LOL, BTW, TTYL, ASAP) out of formal workplace emails.
  • Attachment Cover Context: Never attach a document without explaining what it is in the email body (e.g., "Attached is the revised Q3 budget spreadsheet. Please review rows 14-22...").

3. Group Email Rules: Thoughtful Distribution

Pfeiffer outlines two vital rules when addressing groups:

  • Give Readers Individual Response Instructions: When emailing a team, specify clearly who needs to take action and who is just informed. If you need a response from one person, address them directly in the first line.
  • Suppress Addresses Using BCC (Blind Courtesy Copy): When sending announcements to external clients or large groups:
    • Place the recipient list in BCC rather than TO or CC.
    • Reason 1 (Privacy): Prevents exposing confidential client email addresses to each other (GDPR/privacy violation).
    • Reason 2 (Prevents Reply-All Chaos): Stops recipients from accidentally triggering an annoying "Reply All" flood to hundreds of people.

4. The 7 Cs of Business Communication

The gold standard checklist for evaluating any piece of correspondence:

1. Clear

Simple words, short sentences, and explicit main points so the reader never has to guess your intent.

2. Concise

Eliminate wordy fluff, deadwood, and redundancies (e.g., replace "due to the fact that" with "because").

3. Concrete

Use specific numbers, exact dates, and verified facts instead of vague generalizations ("by 4:00 PM on Friday" vs "soon").

4. Correct

Accurate technical figures, proper spelling, grammatical precision, and correct formatting.

5. Coherent

Logical sequence of ideas connected with transitional phrases; flows naturally from Abstract to Body to Conclusion.

6. Complete

Answers all the reader's questions and provides all background facts necessary for an immediate decision.

7. Courteous

Adopts the "You" attitude, shows genuine respect, avoids accusatory phrasing, and projects positive goodwill.

Key Takeaways

  • Treat every workplace email as a permanent public document.
  • Subject lines must combine Topic + Action/Focus.
  • Suppress mass email distribution lists with BCC to protect privacy and prevent reply-all storms.
  • Verify your draft against the 7 Cs before sending.

Interactive Practice: Tone & Format Drills

Exam Questions Bank
Q1: What are the structural differences between Modified Block and Full Block formats?

Answer:
In Full Block format, every single line—including the date, inside address, salutation, body paragraphs, complimentary close, and signature—starts flush against the left margin. It typically uses open or mixed punctuation.

In Modified Block format, the inside address and body paragraphs remain flush left, but the Date, Complimentary Close, and Signature Block start at the horizontal midpoint (tabbed to center). It typically uses mixed punctuation (colon after salutation, comma after close).

Q2: Explain the difference in punctuation between Open Punctuation and Mixed Punctuation.

Answer:
• Open Punctuation: Omits all punctuation after both the salutation (e.g., Dear Mr. Smith) and the complimentary close (e.g., Sincerely).
• Mixed Punctuation: Uses a colon (:) after the salutation (e.g., Dear Mr. Smith:) and a comma (,) after the complimentary close (e.g., Sincerely,). Mixed punctuation is standard for formal American business correspondence.

Q3: Why is an indirect organizational pattern required for negative correspondence?

Answer:
When readers receive disappointing news directly at the start of a letter, they experience immediate psychological rejection and frustration. As a result, they often stop reading and ignore subsequent explanations. By using the indirect pattern (Buffer → Reasons First → Diplomatic Refusal → Alternative → Goodwill), the writer ensures the reader understands the objective reasons, legal policies, or financial facts before the bad news is revealed, keeping the door open for future business.

Q4: Identify three reasons why BCC should be used when emailing large external client lists.

Answer:
1. Confidentiality / Privacy: Prevents disclosing proprietary customer email lists to competitors or third parties (compliance with privacy regulations).
2. Spam Prevention: Protects client inboxes from being scraped by malware or bad actors on the thread.
3. Prevents "Reply All" Storms: Ensures that if one client replies, their message only goes to the sender rather than broadcasting to all recipients.

Module Summary Checklist

  • Master the 10 standard parts of a business letter and their exact placements.
  • Differentiate Full Block, Modified Block, and Semi-Block letter layouts.
  • Use the standard 4-line header (DATE, TO, FROM, SUBJECT) for all internal memos.
  • Apply the indirect pattern (buffer & reasons first) for all bad news messages.
  • Verify documents against the 7 Cs of business communication.
