Adversarial Search & Game Playing

1Foundations of Game Playing in AI

Navigating competitive multi-agent environments with hostile adversaries

In standard search problems (such as the 8-puzzle, pathfinding, or TSP), the search space is non-hostile and cooperative. The goal state simply sits passively in the graph waiting to be reached; the world does not plot against the agent or alter the state of the board while the agent deliberates.

In Adversarial Search (Game Playing), the agent operates in a competitive multi-agent environment against an intelligent, hostile opponent whose express objective is to prevent our agent from achieving its goal and maximize their own win probability.

Definition • Two-Player Zero-Sum Game
The classical games studied in AI (Chess, Checkers, Go, Tic-Tac-Toe, Nim) are formalized as:
  • Deterministic: No chance elements (no dice rolls or shuffling). Every action leads to a predictable outcome.
  • Two Players: Traditionally designated as MAX (our agent, seeking to maximize the outcome) and MIN (the opponent, seeking to minimize MAX's outcome).
  • Turn-Taking: Players strictly alternate making moves.
  • Perfect Information (Fully Observable): Both players possess complete visibility over the entire board state at all times (contrasted with Poker or Kriegspiel).
  • Zero-Sum: The utility payoff values at the end of the game are equal and opposite: Utility(MAX) + Utility(MIN) = 0. What is good for MAX is equally detrimental to MIN!

Move Generator vs. Evaluator (Lecture 9 Slides 4–7)

Why can't we simply apply pure "generate-and-test" to game playing? Lecture 9 highlights two flawed extremes:

  • Extreme 1 (Generate All Full Games): Generate complete sequences of moves down to terminal leaf nodes, then pick the winning path. Failure: In chess, this requires analyzing 10120 paths. The computation would outlast the universe.
  • Extreme 2 (Greedy 1-Ply Move): Generate all immediate 1-step moves, evaluate each greedily, and pick the best. Failure: The agent is completely incapable of tactical sacrifice (e.g., sacrificing a pawn or knight now to achieve checkmate two turns later).

The Solution: A two-stage balanced architecture:

  1. Plausible Move Generator: Eliminates suicidal, pointless, or illegal moves so that time is not squandered evaluating rubbish states.
  2. Heuristic State Evaluator: Applied to candidate states reached by a limited lookahead search to accurately assess positional and material strength.

2The Game of Nim (Nim-7 Case Study)

Exhaustive game tree analysis from George F. Luger (Chapter 4.4.1)

To understand the mechanics of game trees before dealing with intractable games like chess, George F. Luger uses the classic subtraction/division game of Nim (with 7 tokens).

Rules of Nim-7 (Luger Section 4.4.1 & Lecture 9 Slide 4)
  • 7 tokens (or matchsticks) are initially placed on a table in a single pile: [7].
  • A legal move consists of taking any single pile and dividing it into two non-empty piles of unequal sizes.
  • For example, a pile of 6 tokens may be divided into 5-1 or 4-2, but cannot be split into 3-3 (equal sizes are illegal).
  • A pile of 2 tokens can only be split into 1-1 (illegal, because sizes are equal). Therefore, piles of size 1 and 2 can never be divided further!
  • Winning/Losing Condition: The first player who can no longer make a legal move loses the game!

The Complete Game Tree for Nim-7

Let Player 1 be MIN and Player 2 be MAX. MIN moves first from the initial state [7]:

Ply Depth Turn Board State(s) Generated Legal Move Options
Root (Ply 0) Start [7] MIN must choose from: [5, 2], [6, 1], or [4, 3].
Ply 1 MAX From [5, 2]: splits 5 into [4, 1, 2] or [3, 2, 2].
From [6, 1]: splits 6 into [5, 1, 1] or [4, 2, 1].
From [4, 3]: splits 4 into [3, 1, 3] or 3 into [4, 2, 1]. 		MAX seeks to branch to a winning configuration.
Ply 2 MIN Successor piles such as [3, 2, 1, 1], [2, 2, 2, 1], [4, 1, 1, 1]. Piles shrink toward indivisible states (1s and 2s).
Ply 3 to 5 Alternating Terminal states: [2, 2, 1, 1, 1], [2, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1]. No pile has 3 or more tokens with unequal partition options.
Assigning Payoffs & Backing Up Values
  • Leaf states where MAX has won are assigned a value of 1.
  • Leaf states where MIN has won are assigned a value of 0.
  • The Backing Up Rule:
    • At a MAX node, the backed-up score is max(children).
    • At a MIN node, the backed-up score is min(children).
  • Result for Nim-7: The root node evaluates to 1 for MAX! This proves that Player 2 (MAX) has a guaranteed winning strategy regardless of what Player 1 (MIN) plays, provided MAX plays optimally!

3The Minimax Search Algorithm

Optimal decision strategy in zero-sum adversarial games

The Minimax Algorithm provides an optimal decision strategy for two-player zero-sum games assuming both players play rationally.

Game Terminology: Ply vs. Move
  • Ply: A single turn taken by one player (e.g., White moves a pawn).
  • Move: A complete round consisting of two plies (one ply by Player 1 followed by one ply by Player 2).

Mathematical Formulation of Minimax Value

MINIMAX(s) = egin{cases} Utility(s) & if Terminal(s) \ maxa in Actions(s) MINIMAX( Result(s, a)) & if Player(s) = MAX \ mina in Actions(s) MINIMAX( Result(s, a)) & if Player(s) = MIN end{cases}

Luger's Minimax Algorithm (Formal Pseudocode)

function MINIMAX(state, depth, is_max_player)
    if depth = 0 or state is Terminal then
        return STATIC_EVALUATION(state)
        
    if is_max_player then
        best_val := -∞
        for each child in GENERATE_SUCCESSORS(state) do
            val := MINIMAX(child, depth - 1, false)
            best_val := max(best_val, val)
        return best_val
    else
        best_val := +∞
        for each child in GENERATE_SUCCESSORS(state) do
            val := MINIMAX(child, depth - 1, true)
            best_val := min(best_val, val)
        return best_val

Properties & Complexity of Minimax

  • Completeness: Yes, if the game tree is finite.
  • Optimality: Yes, against an optimal opponent. (If the opponent is sub-optimal, minimax is still safe, but not exploitative).
  • Time Complexity: O(bm), where b is the legal branching factor and m is the maximum depth of the tree.
  • Space Complexity: O(b · m) for a depth-first implementation that generates one path at a time.

4Fixed-Ply Lookahead & Static Evaluation Functions

Approximating deep game states when complete trees cannot be expanded

In non-trivial games like chess or checkers, expanding the game tree to terminal leaf nodes is physically impossible. Therefore, practical game-playing programs search to a fixed lookahead depth (e.g., n=4 plies) and apply a Static Heuristic Evaluation Function to score the frontier states.

Luger's Tic-Tac-Toe Heuristic Evaluation (Luger Chapter 4.4.2 & Lecture 9 Slide 8)

Let player MAX be 'X' and player MIN be 'O'. How do we score a non-terminal board position?

E(n) = M(n) - O(n)
  • M(n): The number of complete winning lines (rows, columns, diagonals) that are still open to MAX (i.e., lines containing no 'O' marks).
  • O(n): The number of complete winning lines that are still open to MIN (i.e., lines containing no 'X' marks).

If E(n) > 0, the state favors MAX. If E(n) < 0, it favors MIN. If E(n) = 0, both players have equal opportunity.

Weighted Collinear Feature Scoring (Lecture 9 Slide 8)

To account for immediate win threats and tactical advantages, FAST-NUCES Lecture 9 defines explicit weighted scores for Tic-Tac-Toe states:

Board Pattern Points Awarded Meaning
Two collinear marks with 3rd space empty +200 points Unblocked 2-in-a-row (imminent win threat).
Two nearly complete lines +300 points Double threat / fork (guaranteed win).
Complete line (3-in-a-row) +600 points Decisive terminal win.
Open potential lines +1 point per line Positional freedom (M(n)).
Worked Slide Example (Lecture 9 Slide 9)

Suppose Player MAX ('X') evaluates 5 candidate successor moves. For each state, we compute:

Total Score = ( MAX Score) - ( MIN Score)

Candidate Move MAX Feature Sum MIN Threat Penalty Net Backed-up Score
Move 1 (Corner) 200 + 5 = 205 -1 204
Move 2 (Edge) 200 + 4 = 204 -2 202
Move 3 (Edge) 200 + 4 = 204 -1 203
Move 4 (Edge) 200 + 4 = 204 -1 203
Move 5 (Corner) 200 + 4 = 204 -1 203

Decision: Move 1 gives the highest evaluation (204), and is selected by MAX!

5Alpha-Beta Pruning Procedure

Eliminating irrelevant game branches without altering the final minimax decision

Minimax searches every single state in the lookahead tree (O(bm)). However, many of these branches are completely irrelevant: once an opponent has proven they have a counter-move that makes a branch worse than an already established option, there is zero need to investigate what else that branch could do!

Alpha-Beta Pruning (Luger Section 4.4.3 & Reference Lecture 8) cuts away these useless subtrees while guaranteeing to compute the exact same minimax value as full search.

The Two Bounds: α and β

  • α (Alpha): The value of the best choice found so far along the path for MAX.
    • α represents the lower bound on the final score that MAX can achieve.
    • MAX will never accept anything worse than α.
    • α is initialized to −∞ and can only increase (never decreases).
  • β (Beta): The value of the best choice found so far along the path for MIN.
    • β represents the upper bound on the final score that MIN can achieve.
    • MIN will never allow MAX to get anything better than β.
    • β is initialized to +∞ and can only decrease (never increases).

The Two Pruning Conditions

  1. α-Cutoff (Pruning at a MIN Node):

    At any MIN node, if the backed-up temporary value becomes less than or equal to α (value ≤ α):

    Prune all remaining unexamined children of this MIN node!
    Reasoning: MAX already has an established alternative path elsewhere that guarantees at least α. Since MIN will pick at most this new lower value, MAX will never choose to enter this branch. Exploring further children of this MIN node is a complete waste of time.

  2. β-Cutoff (Pruning at a MAX Node):

    At any MAX node, if the backed-up temporary value becomes greater than or equal to β (val ge β):

    Prune all remaining unexamined children of this MAX node!
    Reasoning: MIN already has an established counter-move above that holds MAX down to β. Since MAX will choose at least this new higher value, MIN will never allow play to reach this state. Exploring further children of this MAX node is useless.

Worked Step-by-Step Trace from Lecture 10 (Slide 14)

Consider a 2-ply game tree where root node S (MAX) has children A (MIN) and B (MIN).

Step Node Visited Type Current (alpha, β) Value Computed Action & Pruning Decision
1 S MAX [−∞, +∞] - Descend into left child A.
2 A MIN [−∞, +∞] - Descend into first child F (MAX).
3 F MAX [−∞, +∞] - Inspect leaves: Child G evaluates to 2, Child H evaluates to 3. max(2, 3) = 3.
4 A MIN α = −∞, β = 3 min = 3 Value 3 propagates up to MIN node A. β(A) = 3. Temporary ceiling for A is 3.
5 S MAX α = 3, β = +∞ max ≥ 3 A finishes with value 3. Root S updates its lower bound: α = max(−∞, 3) = 3. Now explore right child B.
6 B MIN α = 3, β = +∞ - Descend into first child I (MAX).
7 I MAX α = 3, β = +∞ 5 Leaf I evaluates to 5. MAX node I gets value ≥ 5. Propagate 5 to MIN node B: β(B) ≤ 5.
8 B MIN α = 3, β ≤ 2 (from next child) 2 Next child evaluates to 2 &implies; β(B) = 2.
Condition Checked: β(B) = 2 ≤ α(S) = 3!
PRUNING TRIGGERED: Prune all remaining siblings of node B! Root S ignores B and picks A with guaranteed score 3.

6Move Ordering, Quiescence & The Horizon Effect

Computational efficiency and strategic pitfalls

The Critical Impact of Move Ordering

The efficiency of Alpha-Beta pruning depends entirely on the order in which successor nodes are examined:

Ordering Scenario Effective Branching Factor Time Complexity Search Depth Achievable
Worst-Case Ordering
(Worst moves examined first)		 b (no pruning occurs) O(bm) Same depth as standard Minimax (d).
Random Move Ordering ≈ b3/4 O(b3m/4) ≈ 33% deeper search.
Best-Case Ordering
(Best moves examined first)		 √b O(bm/2) Doubles the search depth! (2d)
The Square-Root Power of Perfect Pruning
In the best case, Alpha-Beta pruning reduces the branching factor from b to √b. For example, in chess where b ≈ 36, optimal move ordering reduces the effective branching factor to √ = 6! This allows the computer to search twice as deep in the exact same amount of time.

The Horizon Effect & Quiescence Search

When search is cut off at an arbitrary fixed depth (e.g., ply 4), the AI can fall victim to the Horizon Effect:

The Horizon Effect
The Horizon Effect occurs when an inevitable, catastrophic loss (such as losing a Queen) is pushed just beyond the search horizon by making a sequence of useless stalling moves (checks or pawn sacrifices). The computer thinks it avoided the catastrophe simply because the capture will now happen at ply 5 instead of ply 3!

Remedy: Quiescence Search
Do not apply static evaluation functions to "turbulent" or "noisy" positions (states involving active captures, checks, or promotions). Instead, continue searching tactical moves until the board reaches a quiescent (stable, quiet) state before evaluating.

7Summary & Tricky Practice Questions

High-yield mastery check for exam preparation

Lectures 9 & 10 Key Takeaways

  • Zero-Sum Games: Utility is strictly equal and opposite. MAX maximizes; MIN minimizes.
  • Nim-7: 7 tokens, split into unequal non-empty piles. Root evaluates to 1 for MAX; Player 2 has a forced win.
  • Minimax: Guarantees optimal play against an optimal opponent. Complexity O(bm) time, O(b · m) space.
  • Evaluation Functions: E(n) = M(n) - O(n) estimates positional lines. Weighted collinear scores reflect immediate win threats.
  • Alpha-Beta: α (MAX lower bound), β (MIN upper bound). Prune when β ≤ α. Reduces best-case complexity to O(bm/2).
  • Horizon Effect: Stalling moves hide inevitable losses beyond the search depth; mitigated by Quiescence Search.
High-Yield Exam Practice Questions
Question 1 (Tricky): Does Alpha-Beta Pruning ever produce a different move or game score than standard Minimax?

Answer: No, never! Alpha-Beta pruning is mathematically guaranteed to return the exact same minimax value and optimal move as full exhaustive minimax. It only prunes branches that can be proven to have zero influence on the final root decision.

Question 2 (Conceptual): At a MIN node with 4 children, the first child evaluates to 3. The ancestor MAX node currently has α = 4. How many more children of this MIN node will be evaluated?

Answer: Zero more children! The MIN node immediately takes a value ≤ 3. Since this is le alpha (3 ≤ 4), an α-cutoff occurs instantly. All 3 remaining children are pruned without evaluation.

Question 3 (Critical Thinking): If an opponent plays sub-optimally (makes mistakes), will Minimax perform worse than if the opponent played perfectly?

Answer: Minimax will still achieve at least the minimax value (and often much better). However, minimax is not exploitative: it conservatively assumes the opponent will always make the best possible counter-move, rather than setting traps that exploit the opponent's known weaknesses.

Question 4 (Analytical): Why is it impossible to split a pile of 2 tokens in the game of Nim?

Answer: In Nim, a move requires dividing a pile into two non-empty piles of different (unequal) sizes. A pile of 2 can only be divided into 1 + 1. Because both piles have the exact same size (1 = 1), this move is strictly illegal by the rules of Nim. Therefore, piles of size 1 and 2 are dead ends.
